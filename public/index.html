How it goes

Five minutes of questions. Ninety days of steps.

  1. Say hi.

    That is the whole sign-up. No form, no app, no password.

  2. Twelve questions, about five minutes.

    What to call you, how money comes in, what you want to fix, what is in the way, who depends on you, roughly what comes in, who you owe, what you could reach tomorrow, what cover you have.

  3. Charles reads your money back. You confirm it.

    One plain sentence, established once and reused, never re-read from a voice note that might have misheard you.

  4. You pick the goal, then the rule.

    Three genuinely different goals your answers support, with what each is worth in your numbers. Then three one-sentence rules of thumb, shown next to what usually happens instead. Charles recommends one of each and says why.

  5. The plan arrives as one document.

    Then the first step, on WhatsApp, with a Done button.

Before any of that: a hardship check.

If you cannot cover food, rent or transport this month, or you are borrowing to eat, Charles does not plan. It gives three moves, names the free help, and pauses with a way back.

Why it is built this way

Tips don't work. Rules and follow-through do.

Tips have been tried.

South Africa has had a national financial-education policy, a Financial Literacy Month and a decade of bank money tips. About half of adults still score as financially literate, and that number has not moved. People know a mashonisa is expensive. What they lack is the next step, in their numbers, this week, and someone who asks on Friday whether it happened.

FSCA baseline, about 51%: draft National Consumer Financial Education Policy, 2026.

Rules of thumb beat lessons.

A randomised trial compared standard accounting training with a simple rule-of-thumb course. The rules improved money habits where the training did nothing, and the gains were largest for the people who started weakest.

Drexler, Fischer & Schoar, Keeping It Simple, AEJ: Applied, 2014.

Menus hurt the weakest.

A Harvard Business School field experiment gave 640 Kenyan entrepreneurs a WhatsApp AI that offered three to five suggestions per question. No average effect. The strongest gained; the weakest did about 10% worse, because they could not tell which suggestion fitted them.

Otis, Clarke, Delecourt, Holtz & Koning, HBS Working Paper 24-042.

So Charles gives one recommendation and follows it up. Never a menu. Never a loan.

The promises

Written into the code, and tested.

  • It never recommends credit.

    No loan, no buy-now-pay-later, no cash advance, however cheap. If you are weighing one, Charles works through your numbers and says the downside first.

  • It never asks for your ID number, account number, card, PIN, OTP or password.

    And says so in the first message. It reads no app and no account. A bank statement you choose to send is read once, the totals and who gets paid are kept, and never the account number or the file.

  • One recommendation, never a menu.

    Charles picks, says why, and says what would change the pick.

  • Every figure is dated.

    A fee, a rule, a grant amount, a deadline: each comes from a checked source with its date. Past 90 days, Charles says “confirm first”.

  • It never claims you said something you did not.

    The plan only ever repeats what you told Charles, confirmed by you.

  • Free and reversible before anything that costs.

    In every recommendation. In hardship, nothing paid is suggested at all.

  • “Forget my money” deletes everything.

    One message, any time, and every row Charles holds about you is gone.

  • Your own AI can connect.

    ChatGPT or Claude can read the same plan and memory. Anything it wants to change is confirmed with you on WhatsApp first.

Charles teaches and plans with you. It is financial education, not financial advice. It never tells you which product to buy and never says a figure is guaranteed.

For the bank

Why put this on a bank's WhatsApp number.

Content was the right instinct, with the wrong unit.

Tips by SMS and a coaching app reach everyone and change nobody. Charles works one household at a time: a step, a how-to, a “done”, a “did it help”, on the number your customers already have.

The rails already exist.

“Move R50 to a savings pocket on grant day” is a thing the bank's own app does, for free. Charles turns what you have built into habits. It names a free, reversible product of yours where it fits. It never pushes a loan.

A stabilised customer can later be lent to responsibly.

Arrears cleared, a cushion growing on payday, a record of behaviour. Charles promises no outcome; the measure we would propose is yours, and the Kenya evidence says the first thing to prove is that the weakest do no worse.

Doing no harm is the feature.

Hardship mode pushes no product, names the free debt counsellor and the ombud, and pauses the plan. That is what earns the trust a lending relationship needs.
